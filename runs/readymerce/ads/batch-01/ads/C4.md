The login page told me to contact an administrator. I read it three times, because the administrator was supposed to be me.

It was 11:40 on a Tuesday night. I was at the kitchen table with a cup of tea gone cold, typing the same password into the same box, and the same grey line kept coming back. Contact an administrator.

I had paid $4,500 for that store. I had the receipts in a manila folder, and a three-week-old email from the man who built it: "almost done, you're going to love it." I'd told my sister it would be live by the weekend.

I tried the help chat. I couldn't even speak to anyone. As far as the system could tell, I wasn't anyone. I didn't know then that the explanation would come from the woman who does my taxes.

My sister texted at 11:52. "Did it go live?" I typed "not yet" and deleted it. Then I typed "I think I got scammed" and sent that instead.

She called right away. She wasn't mean about it, which somehow made it worse. "They're all scams," she said. "Every one of them. Please don't pay another person to build you anything."

I said okay, and I meant it. I closed the laptop and sat with my hand flat on the lid, thinking I'd just flushed the money and the time down the drain, and the hope with it.

For most of a year I agreed with her. I scrolled past every ad with a laptop in it. I was afraid to ever try again.

She was wrong about one thing, though. It took me eleven months to find out which.

I should back up.

The $4,500 builder was my second try.

The first was a store program that cost nothing up front. I thought I'd just link into the store once it was finished.

What I got was a half-empty theme and a stream of emails about the next thing to buy: $97 for an ad pack that never showed up, promises of more templates and products, all after I'd paid.

One night I typed into a forum, "they said they would build a store, but i am building the store???" Someone replied that my store was half-done. I drew a line under it and moved on.

The second time, I paid a real person with a real phone number, because I'd decided the first one failed for being cheap. He sent mockups and a logo. Then the replies slowed, then "almost done," then the login page.

Somewhere in between, a marketing guy took a deposit to "get traffic to it" and never even scheduled a call with me.

I added it up once, on the inside flap of the folder, in pen. $4,597, not counting the marketing guy, because I couldn't make myself write that one down.

The money was bad. What it did to me was worse.

I'm the one in my family who reads the terms, and I'd been taken twice, maybe three times, by people who smiled at me on video calls. I felt stupid in a way I hadn't since school.

Here's the strange part. Even while I was telling my sister they were all scams, I kept checking.

I read every review. I asked for the full contract before any call. When one company did call, I Googled them while the man was still talking. The pitch was almost always the same.

If I truly believed they were all scams, why was I still checking? Nobody reads reviews of something they think is always a lie. Part of me knew some were real. I just had no way to tell them apart.

So I started digging. Late at night I read forum posts from people who'd paid someone to build a store and ended up where I did. "When the store was transferred to me, the store was shutdown almost immediately," one woman wrote. She was in tears.

I kept a note on my phone of every story: the price, the promises, the reviews. I was hunting for the red flag I should have caught. The note never showed a pattern, because I was writing down the wrong things.

The woman who does my taxes works above a dry cleaner. In February I brought her the folder with everything else.

She flipped through the receipts. Then she asked a question nobody had asked me in two years, including me.

"Whose name is the store account in?"

I started to say mine. Then I thought about that login page.

"I don't actually know," I said.

She nodded like she'd heard it before. "Then it isn't your store yet. It's his, and you're a guest in it." Then she asked a second question. "After it went live, who was supposed to be working on it?"

Nobody.

Three different people had my money, and not one of them ever mentioned whose name the store would be in, or who'd still be there after launch day. They were all paid to get it to launch day. None of them were paid for the day after.

That night I read the fine print. Not the builders'. The platform's.

Its own documentation for people who build stores for clients says it in one sentence: "You own and configure the store during the build phase, then transfer ownership to the client when it's ready to go live."

So while the store is being built, it belongs to the builder. It becomes yours at go-live, which is also the moment the builder's job is designed to end.

Two things had gone wrong, and neither was me being stupid. My store sat in someone else's account, so when he stopped answering, I was locked out of my own work.

And his job ended on launch day, exactly when a new store needs someone reading the first results, fixing listings and testing the next product.

It's like paying someone to build a house on your land, letting them keep the deed until move-in day, then watching them drive off that same afternoon, right when you find out which pipes leak.

I hadn't failed those stores. They were set up to fail me at the handover.

And my sister was wrong about one thing. They weren't all scams. The ones that burned me had the same two holes, and I could check for both before I paid anyone.

On my way out, she added, "One of my clients had hers built this spring. It was in her name from the first day. I've seen her statements. The payouts go straight to her bank." She wrote a name on a sticky note. Readymerce.

I put the sticky note in the folder and didn't touch it for two weeks.

When I finally looked, I Googled them. Almost nothing: a new site, no reviews I could read. A year earlier, that alone would have ended it.

But I wasn't shopping for a pitch anymore. I needed two answers in writing.

The first answer was on their page in plain words: "The store account is in your name and stays in your name." Right under it: "Payouts go to your bank account."

The second answer was further down the same page. After the store goes live, their list says products go out in stages, each with a small ad test on a budget you approve. Then an early performance review, post-launch corrections, new products rolled in.

How much ongoing work you get is agreed on a call, so I asked on the call and wrote down every word.

Then I went looking for the catch. What I found was a list of what the $500 doesn't cover: product costs, platform fees, ad spend, and any promise of income. Nobody who'd pitched me had ever written down what they wouldn't do.

I read the refund policy twice. The $500 fee is refundable for 7 days from payment, and that ends the moment you accept delivery, buy any add-on, or ask for more work. Platform fees, ad spend, domain, apps and supplier costs are never refunded.

Narrower than I'd like. But it was the first refund anyone had explained to me before I paid.

I still waited until the $500 and an ad budget I could afford to lose were actually in my account, not borrowed. Then I paid.

The day the store opened, I didn't wait for an email. I logged in myself, at the same kitchen table.

No grey line. The owner field had my name in it.

I sat there longer than I'd admit.

The first products went live in stages. Each got a small ad test, on a budget I approved myself.

Then the first review came, in writing: which products to keep, which to replace, what they were changing on the listings. After launch day, they were still there. That was the part I had never once had.

My sister came over on a Sunday and asked, carefully, the way you'd ask about an ex, "So is it live?"

I turned the laptop around. She read the owner field out loud, and it was my name.

She was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Okay. Show me the review."

I showed her. And for the first time in two years, talking about the store didn't make my face go hot.

The folder is still in my kitchen drawer. I don't open it at night anymore.

If I could talk to the woman at that kitchen table, I'd tell her she was never stupid. She was sold a finish line.

So here's all I'd tell anyone holding a folder like mine. Paying someone to build it wasn't your mistake. Buying a build that ended on launch day, inside someone else's account, was.

Before you pay anyone, get two answers in writing. Who is still working on it after launch day? And whose name is it in?

If the money isn't there yet, wait. I did.

When it is, the only right answer to that second question is three words long.

In your name.
